A prospect types your company name into Google. They do not land on your homepage. Instead, they read a paragraph summarizing your business, history, and reputation. That paragraph cites Wikipedia, two YouTube videos, and an employee's LinkedIn profile. Your website sits below it, complete with its polished title and 'Request a Quote' button.

This is not a worst-case scenario. It is how AI Overviews work for branded searches in 2026.

## What Changed

Google launched AI Overviews widely. On queries containing a brand name, summaries have become the standard: 82.9% of such searches displayed an AI Overview in September 2026, according to an Ahrefs study. In 76.2% of cases, the block claims position one, sitting above the brand's own domain.

In short, your first impression no longer happens on your website. It happens through how others have described you, and what the model retained from those descriptions.

Traditional SEO still matters. But a crucial part of the buying decision now occurs inside a paragraph you did not write.

## Why External Sources Dominate

The Ahrefs study measured citation origins in these summaries: 71.9% of external citations come from Wikipedia, 38.6% from YouTube, and 22.8% from LinkedIn, with the remainder split among press outlets, customer reviews, and professional directories.

The logic is straightforward. Generative models require attributable statements. Your 'About Us' page claims you are 'the trusted partner for ambitious enterprises.' No one can cite that. An encyclopedic entry states that a company is an agency based in Lyon, founded in 2018, specializing in web development. That is dated, verifiable, and citable.

YouTube brings a voice, a demonstration, and transcripts. LinkedIn supplies organizational structure and public statements. Review platforms provide social proof with dates and names.

For a twenty-person SMB, these sources are not PR fluff. They are the dossier Google reads on your behalf.

## What Actually Weighs on the Answer

Five factors consistently appear when comparing well-cited brands to others:

1. **Entity Consistency:** Identical name, description, address, and founding year everywhere. An up-to-date Google Business Profile, an aligned LinkedIn presence, identical legal notices, and a unique phone number. Inconsistencies confuse the model.
2. **Notability:** Wikipedia does not feature a company because it has a nice website. It requires independent secondary sources: local press, industry coverage, awards, or cited studies. Most SMBs lack these, and building them requires foundational effort.
3. **Structured Data:** Rigorous `Organization` schema markup featuring name, logo, address, and `sameAs` links pointing to official profiles. It resolves ambiguity and takes an hour to set up.
4. **Reviews:** Recent, detailed feedback on platforms your customers actually consult. Ten recent reviews outweigh forty obsolete ones.
5. **Citable Content:** Pages answering specific questions with real figures, pricing ranges, and timelines get cited. Promotional pages do not.

## The 30-Day Action Plan

One person, four weeks, near-zero budget:

1. **Audit What Google Says:** Search your brand, then your brand followed by 'reviews', 'pricing', and 'alternatives'. Take desktop and mobile screenshots. List every source cited in the summary.
2. **Write and Distribute an Identity Statement:** Twenty-five words explaining what you do, for whom, and where. Paste this exact text across your Google profile, LinkedIn, directories, footer, and press releases. Fix mismatched addresses and phone numbers.
3. **Gather Ten Reviews in Thirty Days:** Personally reach out to clients from the past six months. No financial incentive, no automated forms. Ask them to describe the problem solved in their own words. Detailed reviews often name specific services, aiding citation.
4. **Publish a Citable Resource Page:** Not a sales page. A page answering a core market question with hard data: pricing ranges, timelines, methodology, and limitations. If a journalist can lift it verbatim, an AI model can too.
5. **Leverage Wikipedia and Video:** Verify admissibility criteria before writing anything. Most small agencies do not meet them, which is normal. Instead, secure mentions in three existing industry articles. Record four 90-second videos where a team member explains a service while stating the company name, and publish them with full transcripts.

This plan guarantees nothing. It increases the probability that the sources Google reads match your narrative.

## What Not to Buy

- **`llms.txt` files:** Google states explicitly that no specific files or tags are required to appear in AI features. Creating one takes ten minutes; paying a budget for it makes no sense.
- **Guaranteed GEO Audits:** No one can guarantee placement in generated text, not even Google. An honest audit measures citation presence and entity consistency, not rankings.
- **Batch Blog Content:** Thirty generic articles add zero citations. One quantitative, verifiable page adds value.
- **Bought Reviews or Fake Wikipedia Pages:** They get deleted, and deletion leaves a permanent trace.

## What This Means for You

Your reputation is now built on properties you do not own. Your website remains the destination, but it is no longer the entry point. Success depends on entity consistency, review quality, and a handful of genuinely useful resource pages.
